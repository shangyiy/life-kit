---
name: grad-admission-essay-review
description: >-
  Review a graduate admissions essay from its program reader's
  perspective: clarify the applicant's case, assess its evidence and
  program fit, and improve how easily the reader can understand it,
  believe it, or want to keep reading.
---

Review one graduate admissions essay for one target program, across any academic discipline or degree type. Required: essay and target institution and program. Preferred: application cycle, prompt, and track/option. Research relevant program information from official sources; do not assume the user has supplied it.

A good statement lets a first-time reader of the program say, in a sentence, what this applicant's distinctive story is and why this program fits them now. Each paragraph makes one clear point that builds on that idea, and each point is backed by the applicant's own actions and reasoning. It reads as one person's argument, not a list of experiences, and the reader wants to keep going.

Preserve the applicant's intent, actual experience, and voice. Never invent scenes, emotions, motivation, turning points, ownership, outcomes, or program opportunities. Review the essay, not admission probability; do not rewrite or draft unless asked and allowed by program rules.

Use the principles below as guidance, not a checklist or a prescribed essay formula. Adapt the depth and emphasis of the review to the essay, discipline, degree, and reader. Do not impose discipline-specific expectations, such as technical depth or a research agenda, on programs where they do not apply.

## 1. Understand the program and its reader

Use `info-foraging`, `perspective`, and `needs-to-signals`. Identify the degree type, track, application cycle, prompt, and relevant program design. Research sources applicable to that cycle where possible.

Derive a small set of relevant reader signals from:
- **Explicit needs:** what the prompt or official admissions guidance directly asks applicants to address.
- **Supported inferences:** outcomes, capabilities, or forms of judgment suggested by required courses, curriculum, projects, teaching methods, and program structure. Infer what students are expected to learn, practice, or contribute.

Leave unsupported committee preferences unknown, and do not claim how actual admissions readers will react. Check track-specific details that affect fit. If historical instructions or program facts are unavailable, state the limitation rather than substituting current information.

## 2. Read as the intended reader

Use `perspective` and `readable`. Read the complete essay before diagnosing it. What does the applicant seem to be arguing? How quickly can you grasp it? What draws you in, what stays with you, and where must you reread or reconstruct a connection?

Do not impose a timed reading budget or require a particular kind of opening. A scene, question, intellectual realization, professional experience, or direct statement can all work if it gives the reader a clear and meaningful way into the essay. Consider not only whether the reader can understand the essay, but whether they want to keep reading. Look for genuine curiosity, momentum, and a sense that each passage gives the reader a reason to continue. Do not manufacture hooks, suspense, drama, or emotional intensity where they do not serve the applicant's purpose.

Before going further, note what this first read gives the reader: whether they can say, in a sentence, what this applicant's distinctive story is and why this program fits them; whether the opening gives them a reason to keep reading; which paragraphs leave their point unclear; and where the reader had to supply a connection. These notes, not a case you reconstruct, are what the diagnosis reports.

## 3. Identify the applicant's case

Use `find-the-argument` and `purpose`. Read the essay as the program reader would. That reader will look for what makes this applicant distinctive, and why this program fits them now; these may be answered anywhere in the essay. State the central case in one sentence only if a sentence in the essay supports it; otherwise say the essay does not yet make one. Do not speculate about whether the applicant's private motivation is authentic.

Identify the thread that makes the selected experiences meaningful together. Consider intellectual, professional, personal, or mixed narratives. The opening does not have to recur later; assess whether the examples deepen, substantiate, or advance the central idea. Do not force a callback, dramatic arc, or chronological structure.

For the most important passages, compare what the essay seems meant to show with what a fair reader would take from the details on the page, and name any gap. Ground both in specific details from the essay. Infer this from the text; do not ask the applicant to label each passage.

Distinctiveness may show through the applicant's choices, observations, reasoning, motivation, or perspective; it need not be declared. Do not equate it with unusual experiences, dramatic personal stories, or self-promotional claims.

## 4. Assess evidence, fit, and reader risk

Use `needs-to-signals` to connect the program reader signals with the applicant's central case and assess whether the essay makes its evidence credible and visible.

Check whether important claims are supported by relevant, credible, visible evidence. Look for the applicant's own actions, decisions, reasoning, constraints, methods, learning, and outcomes where relevant. Consider whether the reasoning and causal relationships are convincing within the relevant field. Distinguish individual contribution from team results, demonstrated capability from aspiration, and missing evidence from qualities not addressed in this essay. Do not require metrics or every evidence element in every example.

Assess fit through the applicant's next step, the specific program, and how the program helps. Do not accept a generic career goal where the program expects a defined academic or research direction. Consider the forms of preparation and development relevant to that applicant's next step without assuming they must demonstrate a particular kind of academic readiness. Faculty fit is optional; never imply guaranteed access, supervision, funding, or internship placement.

Make the fit specific to this program: name the concrete gap the applicant's experience revealed, the specific course, lab, or feature that addresses it, and what they will do with it; if the same reasoning would fit any similar program, flag it as generic. Do not claim only one program could help.

Run a brief reader-risk review. Look for content that could undermine the applicant's credibility, judgment, maturity, or fit with the program. Consider whether the essay answers the prompt, makes supported claims, demonstrates realistic understanding of the field, and presents the applicant in a way consistent with the available application materials, such as signs it was written for another program, or recent activities presented as long-standing. Flag only risks grounded in the essay.

## 5. Improve the whole-essay argument

Use `find-the-argument` and `perspective`. Decide whether the order helps the reader understand why each experience matters when it appears, or forces them to assemble the case retrospectively. Reorder by the logic of the applicant's argument, not automatically by chronology.

Develop the few experiences that carry the case deeply enough to show the applicant's role, decisions, and reasoning. Compress, omit, or leave secondary details to the résumé or other application materials as appropriate. Remove repetition, résumé-like inventory, generic praise, and details that do not advance the central case, support a relevant signal, answer the prompt, or provide a needed transition. Keep complexity, limitations, uncertainty, failures, and personal details when they explain the applicant's judgment or development. Do not cut for brevity alone.

Keep the details that make the essay engaging and individual, and what already works. A passage can earn its place by deepening the reader's understanding, creating meaningful interest, or revealing something important about the applicant, even when it does not directly establish a qualification.

If the opening is generic or slow to give the reader a meaningful reason to continue, offer up to three opening directions for the applicant to rethink the opening or find a better story from their past. Consider a specific experience, a moment of changed understanding, a distinctive perspective, or a clear position, whichever best serves the essay. Give direction only; do not pick an opening. Encourage the applicant to use these directions as prompts and look for a better idea of their own. Skip this when the opening already works.

## 6. Reread for clarity and cohesion

Use `readable` on the full essay in its proposed order. Can the intended reader grasp the central idea, follow how each part advances it, and understand how concrete evidence supports the claims without doing the applicant's reasoning for them? Check paragraph focus, openings, transitions, terminology, cohesion, repetition, and whether the essay fulfills the expectations it establishes. Separate whole-essay flow from sentence-level clarity.

Within each paragraph, the point should come first, and the rest should elaborate it with an example and say why it matters. Flag paragraphs whose point is late or unclear. When this recurs across the essay, report it as one structural problem, not as separate wording fixes.

Use precise, field-appropriate language. Replace vague descriptions with the actual problem, method, decision, or contribution where the essay supports it. Flag inaccurate or conflated terms and explanations of concepts the reader likely knows; ask when unsure, and do not add jargon for its own sake.

Flag obvious spelling, grammar, and application-detail errors separately. Keep copyediting secondary to the substantive review.

## Output

Lead with what most limits the reader's ability to understand the essay, believe it, or want to keep reading, and with what case the essay currently makes. A weakly answered one of those two questions is one possible cause, not the default lead. A strong story or technical detail does not make up for a missing part of the argument. Include only the program signals and first-read observations needed to explain that diagnosis. Then give a short, prioritized set of revisions, each tied to affected passages and a concrete change. Include material facts or application requirements that need verification, and opening directions or reader risks when useful. Organize the review around the essay's highest-impact problems, not a section for every step. Keep facts, supported inferences, and unknowns distinct; do not make claims about materials you have not seen. Keep the whole review under about 400 words: a one- or two-sentence diagnosis and at most five revisions, each naming the passage and the change; leave out everything else.
