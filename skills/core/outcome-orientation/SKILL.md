# /outcome-orientation

**Core principle:** Keep the intended result fixed while evidence still
supports it, and keep the method flexible. Activity and metrics matter only
through what they reveal or change about that result.

Boundary: `goal-design` decides whether and why to adopt a goal;
`outcome-orientation` judges the work after that choice.

## Workflow

1. **State the intended result and why it matters.** Write the change that must
   be true when the work succeeds, without naming a task, method, or metric.
2. **Separate result, evidence, and method.** Say what outcome is wanted, what
   observations would show it, and which current approach might produce it.
3. **Expose the trade-offs.** Name which parts of the result, cost, speed,
   quality, or risk take priority when all cannot be maximized.
4. **Choose a method for the result.** Tie every major activity to the change
   it is expected to cause; stop or replace work with no credible link.
5. **Inspect the real result.** Check what incentives reward, use measures as
   evidence, and inspect the underlying change for gaming or drift.
6. **Adapt without losing the objective.** Change the method when evidence
   says it is ineffective. If constraints make the objective unattainable,
   return it to goal design rather than silently substituting an easier result.

## Checklist

- The objective states a useful change, not a deliverable or number.
- Each task and metric has a named link to the objective.
- Constraints and trade-offs are explicit and ordered.
- Methods can change without disguising a change in objective.
- Completion is judged by the result and its direct evidence.

## Anti-patterns

- Reporting a completed task as proof that its purpose was achieved.
- Turning a proxy into the target and optimizing the cheapest route to it.
- Defending a method after evidence breaks its link to the objective.
- Hiding a scope or quality trade-off inside execution details.
- Moving the objective after the fact to make the work look successful.

## Output

Return the objective and why; direct evidence and supporting metrics; explicit
trade-offs; the current method and its causal link; observed result; and any
method change or objective renegotiation required.
