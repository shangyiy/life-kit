# /needs-to-signals

The method for deriving what matters to an audience and what observable evidence could show that a need is addressed. Use it to do the work or to review an existing set of criteria. It does not evaluate the subject or issue the final verdict; the consuming workflow owns domain-specific rubrics and thresholds, evaluation of actual evidence, verdicts, and recommendations.

## Principles

1. **Start from the audience's situation.** Use `perspective` (principles 1–2) to see the audience's decision and the need behind each requirement; reuse an existing model, and skip this when needs are explicit. Keep a literal requirement when it is itself consequential; never replace a hard constraint with a guess.
2. **Make each signal observable.** Translate a material need into something a person or artifact could demonstrate. A signal may describe an outcome, capability, decision or trade-off, method, scope, or learning. Prefer concrete capability over generic traits. Do not force every signal into a metric: decisions, reasoning, methods, and outcomes can all be observable. A useful signal should make it possible to say what evidence would support it and what would not.
3. **Keep the signal, evidence, and interpretation distinct.**
   - **Signal:** what matters to the audience.
   - **Evidence:** what could be observed that would support or weaken it.
   - **Interpretation:** what observed evidence supports; the consuming skill does this.

   A signal is not evidence that it has been met.
4. **Do not mistake proxies for proof.** Familiarity, titles, participation and uncontextualized metrics are proxies, not proof; do not infer causation from correlation.
5. **Separate what is known from what is inferred.** Mark each need or signal as:
   - **Explicit:** directly stated in the request, requirements, or source material.
   - **Supported inference:** not directly stated, but reasonably derived from the audience's context. Retain the reasoning behind the inference.
   - **Unknown:** the available context is insufficient to determine it.

   Do not turn unknowns into requirements or present inferred preferences as official criteria. When the available context is insufficient, state what remains unknown instead of filling the gap with assumptions.
6. **Keep only signals that matter.** Keep only signals that change the audience's decision and can be observed; drop generic traits and duplicates.
