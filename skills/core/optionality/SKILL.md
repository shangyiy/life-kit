# /optionality

Find the real choices you have and judge what each is worth.

People often accept a path because they think it is the only one. The five
principles say what a choice is and what gives it worth; the paragraphs after
them say how to put them to use.

## Principles

1. **A choice is a right, not a duty.** It gives you the ability to take a path
   without requiring you to take it. Its value is the difference it makes
   compared with not having it, including the other choices it preserves or
   closes. Once exercised, it becomes a commitment to that path and its
   consequences.
2. **The downside must be bounded.** Count two costs apart. Keeping a choice
   open costs money, time, attention, and what you give up by not committing
   elsewhere; sometimes keeping it pays you instead, as a job with a salary
   does. Exercising it costs whatever you cannot get back once you act. An
   apparent option is not truly optional if exercising it locks you into
   obligations or future losses you cannot bear. Sunk costs may make walking
   away harder psychologically, but only unavoidable future costs and
   commitments should affect the forward decision.
3. **The ceiling is the upside you can realistically capture.** It depends on
   how often and how much good opportunities arise, and on whether you can
   reach, recognize, qualify for, and act on them. Keep the size of an
   opportunity apart from your ability to capture it: a huge upside with no
   plausible path to it means nothing. Skills, capital, credentials,
   relationships, and access raise the ceiling and build up over time, so the
   same path can be worth much more later. You can improve your odds, not
   control the outcome.
4. **The value of keeping a choice open can increase under uncertainty.**
   When waiting provides useful information before an irreversible commitment,
   while the downside remains bounded and the upside remains reachable,
   preserving the choice can be worth more than committing now. Uncertainty
   alone does not make a path valuable.
5. **Time both feeds and drains a choice.** Waiting can add information,
   skills, and resources that make a choice more valuable. But opportunities
   can expire, access can disappear, and the cost of keeping a choice open can
   grow. Compare what waiting may add with what it may consume; when the choice
   loses value faster than you learn or prepare, act or let it go before the
   window closes. Holding a choice is not by itself a reason to use it.

**How to apply.** Scale the depth of analysis to the stakes: use a lightweight
check of cost and reversibility for small choices, and reserve scenarios (a
bad, a typical, and a good outcome with rough odds) for decisions with material
downside or significant upside. Find what is already open, including walking
away. Doing nothing, waiting, and keeping the current state are choices too,
but they are not free: windows and offers expire. Count only real choices: one
that has been checked and is available, will still be there if the trouble that
would make you need it happens, and is one you would use in some outcome you
can picture. Mark any fact not yet checked as unknown, and do not count a
choice that rests on one until it is checked. Then ask, for each path, what you
could still do after taking it: wait, stop partway, expand, or switch. Build a
missing choice with a limited first commitment. An average alone is not
enough: consider whether plausible downside scenarios are survivable, not just
whether the expected outcome looks attractive.

**Boundaries.** Only the person deciding can judge what loss they could bear;
lay out the trade-off and do not decide it for them.
