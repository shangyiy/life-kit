# /optionality

Find the choices, judge what each is worth, and let the person choose.

People often accept a path because they think it is the only one. Seeing the
real choices, and what each is worth, keeps that from happening.

## Find the choices

1. **List what is already open.** Include doing nothing and keeping things as
   they are.
2. **Look for choices you have not seen.** Ask what you would do if this path
   disappeared, what others in your situation chose, and what changes with a
   different place, time, or combination. Treat what others chose as an idea
   to check, not a fact about your case.
3. **Build a choice with a small first investment.** Spend only enough to make
   a new path possible. Use `experimentation` to test it cheaply.

## Judge what each is worth

Worth is a rough judgment, not a number. Use five checks: is it real, how
freely it lets you walk away, what it costs to keep, time, and environment.

4. **Count only real choices.** Count only choices you have checked. A
   fallback lost in the same event that makes you need it is not real. Ask
   whether the person could survive the path failing; they decide what they
   can survive.
5. **Check how freely it lets you walk away.** The better your real
   alternative, the easier it is to refuse an offer or leave where you are.
   It also lets you hear no with less fear, because a no does not leave you
   with nothing. This measures your position; it does not say you should
   leave.
6. **Count what it costs to keep this choice open.** Include money, time,
   attention, relationships, and what you give up to keep it.
7. **Count time.** A choice can expire or cost more later. Wait only when
   waiting brings information.
8. **Check how many chances the environment offers.** The place, team, or
   field you are in sets how many chances arise and how large they are. You
   can choose your odds, not the outcome. Skip this check when the decision
   is too small for it to matter.

## Workflow

Find the choices you already have, those you had not seen, and those you can
build; check each one; then report. Match the depth to the size of the
decision: for a small one, a line per choice is enough.

## Checklist

Did you include doing nothing? Did you search for and build choices? Did
each choice get the checks that apply?

## Anti-patterns

Presenting one path as inevitable; counting an unverified choice; hiding cost;
treating better odds as a promised outcome; choosing for the person; a long
analysis of a small decision.

## Output

List the choices found, each one's worth on the five checks, and the facts
that would change it. If asked what to do, say plainly that the choice is
theirs, then name the facts that would tip it, which path each fact favors,
and the next cheap check.

## Boundaries

A choice is worth keeping open only while what it gives you is more than it
costs to keep; when it is not, committing is the person's call.

`perspective` looks at a situation from the other person's side to win their
support; `optionality` looks at the choices you have. Goals belong to
`goal-design`; motives belong to `motivation`.
