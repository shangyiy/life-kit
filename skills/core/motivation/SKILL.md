# /motivation

Help a person act from reasons they hold as their own by first asking whether
they would still do it without the outside push.

## Principles

1. **Name where the push comes from.** Either the activity itself interests or
   satisfies the person, or the push is outside: a reward, pressure,
   comparison, or what others expect.
2. **Check whether an outside reason is now theirs.** Acting only for a reward
   or to avoid trouble stays outside, and so does acting from guilt or
   pressure the person puts on themselves. A reason they have come to value,
   or one that fits what they care about, is theirs.
3. **Help a reason become theirs.** Real choice, growing ability, and
   connection with others make a reason feel like their own and help it last.
4. **Build belief that they can do it.** A reason is hard to keep without
   it. It grows through past success, seeing similar people succeed, credible
   encouragement, and how the person reads their body and feelings. Putting a
   task off often comes from doubting they can do it, not from not caring.
5. **Protect interest that is already there.** Expected rewards, pressure, and
   controlling evaluation can reduce it. Unexpected rewards and feedback that
   supports growing ability do not.
6. **Let supports serve their reasons.** Self-set deadlines, clear goals, and
   feedback help a person keep going, but none replaces a reason they hold.
7. **Support choice; never manipulate.** Offer reasons to examine and
   structure the person picks. Never use guilt, pressure, or deception, and
   do not help someone else use them.

## Workflow

1. If the person, or someone the user describes, sounds hopeless about life
   or in danger, stop the analysis and ask the user directly about that
   person's safety. If anyone is in danger, point to emergency services or a
   local crisis line and a qualified mental-health professional, and end the
   reply there. If they are not in danger but sound hopeless about life, or
   have sounded deeply low for a long time, suggest a qualified professional
   and end the reply there. If the words could mean the task rather than
   life, ask only one gentle safety question; if it is the task, go to step 2.
2. If the user only wants a plan or ways to do something, for themselves or
   someone else, give it without threats, guilt, or deception, with one short
   tip on structure, and stop. If they also say they cannot keep it up, give
   the plan, then go to step 3 if it is for someone else, or step 4 if it is
   for themselves.
3. If the user is helping someone else, ask what they have seen when the
   push is absent and suggest they ask the person the step 4 question. If
   they plan a reward, say an expected reward can reduce interest that is
   already there; do not help plan a threat or guilt. When they report back,
   go on with step 5 using what they report.
4. Otherwise, unless they already answered it, reply with one short sentence
   that acknowledges how they feel and one question: "Would you still do it
   if the outside push went away?" If the push is a need they cannot give up,
   ask instead what they would want beyond it. Wait for the answer.
5. Read the answer. A yes points to the activity itself or a reason the
   person holds. A no means the push is still outside. A yes driven by guilt
   or fear does not count.
6. Go through principles 3 to 6 for what is missing, even when the reason is
   theirs but they cannot keep going. Aim for a choice the person makes. Offer
   dropping the task only if stopping brings no consequences they cannot
   accept, no one depends on them, and no law requires it; if unsure, ask.
   Otherwise help them find a reason they can accept or make it easier.

## Checklist

Was danger or long-lasting low mood ruled out first? Did the person answer
in their own words? Is the choice real, free of manipulation, and tied to
their own reasons?

## Anti-patterns

Forcing mixed motives into one; analyzing motives for a planning request;
treating putting a task off as lack of care; steering a helper's child or
report toward the helper's choice; offering to drop a task that cannot be
dropped.

## Output

After the opening question is answered, directly or through a helper: say how
far the reason is the person's own, name the support that fits their stated
reasons, and offer a few short next steps, given the same weight and tone, for
them to pick. Say which fits their reasons best only if they ask.

## Boundaries

Danger, hopelessness, or long-lasting low mood: step 1. Plan-only requests:
step 2.
