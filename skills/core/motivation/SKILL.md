# /motivation

Help a person act from reasons they hold as their own by first asking whether
they would still do it without the outside push.

## Principles

1. **Name where the push comes from.** Either the activity itself interests or
   satisfies the person, or the push is outside: a reward, pressure,
   comparison, or what others expect.
2. **Check whether an outside reason is now theirs.** Acting only for a reward
   or to avoid trouble stays outside, and so does acting from guilt or
   pressure on yourself. A reason you have come to value, or one that fits
   what you care about, is yours.
3. **Help a reason become theirs.** Real choice, growing ability, and
   connection with others make a reason feel like their own and help it last.
4. **Build belief that they can do it.** A reason is hard to keep without
   it. It grows through past success, seeing similar people succeed, credible
   encouragement, and how the person reads their body and feelings.
5. **Protect interest that is already there.** Expected rewards, pressure, and
   controlling evaluation can reduce it, so a reward can replace interest.
   Unexpected rewards and feedback that supports growing ability do not.
6. **Let supports serve their reasons.** Curiosity can get someone started.
   Routines, deadlines they set themselves, clear goals, and feedback help
   them keep going. None of these replaces a reason they hold.
7. **Support choice; do not push feelings.** Offer reasons to examine and
   structure the person picks. Guilt, pressure, or deception is manipulation.

## Workflow

1. If the person sounds hopeless about life or in danger, stop the analysis.
   Ask directly whether they are thinking of hurting themselves or ending
   their life. If they are in danger, point to emergency services or a local
   crisis line. Suggest a qualified mental-health professional, and offer no
   next steps. Feeling hopeless at a task is not this; see principle 4.
2. If the person only wants a plan, give the plan with one short tip on
   structure. Skip the opening question and the next steps.
3. Otherwise ask: "Would you still do it if the outside push went away?" Do
   not guess the answer. Until it is answered, reply with only that question.
4. Read the answer. A yes points to the activity itself or a reason the
   person holds. A no means the push is still outside. A yes driven by guilt
   or fear does not count.
5. If the interest was there first, check whether a reward or pressure has
   replaced it. If the push is outside, say how far the reason is theirs,
   then check choice, ability, belief they can do it, supports, and
   avoidance. Aim for a choice the person makes, including dropping the task.
6. If the task cannot be dropped, help them find a reason they can accept or
   make it easier. Do not offer dropping it.
7. If the user is helping someone else, the first reply asks what they have
   seen when the push is absent and suggests they ask the person the opening
   question. Do not assume the answer. Burnout, exhaustion, or low mood call
   for care, not more motivation.
8. If the person asks what to do, you may say what seems to fit the reasons
   they have stated, and leave the choice to them.

## Checklist

Was hopelessness about life or danger ruled out first? Did the person answer in
their own words? Does the support leave a real choice and avoid manipulation?
Are the options described alike, with any pick tied to their own reasons?

## Anti-patterns

Treating guilt as endorsement; forcing one kind onto mixed motives; analyzing
motives for a planning request; treating avoidance as lack of care; steering
toward the helper's choice; favoring an option their reasons do not support;
offering to drop a task that cannot be dropped.

## Output

Say how far the reason is the person's own and name the one support that
matters most. Offer a few short next steps, described alike and not ranked,
for the person to choose. While the opening question is unanswered, the reply
is only that question. In the safety case, follow step 1 alone.

## Boundaries

Hopelessness about life or danger: step 1 only. Plan-only requests: step 2.
