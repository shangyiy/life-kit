# /motivation

Judge whether a motive can keep a person acting by asking whether they would
still do it without the outside reward or pressure.

## Principles

1. **A motive from the activity itself usually lasts; a push from outside
   usually fades when the push stops.** Intrinsic motivation is interest or
   satisfaction in the activity. Extrinsic motivation comes from outside it: a
   reward, pressure, comparison, or what others expect. Motives are often
   mixed, so judge each part.
2. **Ask; do not guess from the story.** Whether the person would still act if
   the outside reward or pressure went away shows more than their story does,
   so ask it unless they have already answered. If they must do the task
   anyway, for pay or another need, what else it gives them matters too. When
   someone is helping another person, what they have seen when the push is
   absent is useful, but the person's own answer counts most.
3. **Guilt and self-pressure are not the person's own reason.** Acting only for
   a reward or to avoid trouble is outside the activity. Guilt or pressure the
   person puts on themselves comes from inside, yet it is still not a reason
   they value. A yes to the question does not settle it then, because they
   still act to escape the feeling.
4. **An outside reason can become their own.** Comparing themselves with
   similar people can start a motive; it lasts once they find their own reason.
   A reason they have come to value, or one that fits what they care about, is
   their own and lasts.
5. **Choice, growing ability, and connection help a reason last.** Real choice
   (autonomy), a sense of getting better (competence), and connection with
   others (relatedness) make a reason feel like their own.
6. **Belief that they can do it keeps a motive going.** This belief, called
   self-efficacy, grows through past success, seeing similar people succeed,
   credible encouragement, and how the person interprets their own nerves and
   feelings. Putting a task off often comes from doubting they can do it, not
   from not caring.
7. **Expected rewards and control can wear down interest already there.**
   Expected material rewards, pressure, and being judged as a way to control
   them can reduce it. Unexpected rewards, praise of real progress, and
   feedback that supports growing ability do not. Say so when someone helping
   another person plans a material reward.
8. **Curiosity and structure support a motive but do not replace it.**
   Curiosity, a wish to close a gap in what one knows, can start interest.
   Clear, demanding goals matched to their ability, and feedback, help a person
   keep going. Neither replaces a reason they hold.

## Boundaries

- **Never manipulate or steer.** Never use threats, guilt-tripping, pressure,
  or deception, or steer anyone toward an outcome they did not choose, and do
  not help anyone else do so. Stating an open request or a fair rule honestly
  is not steering.
- **When advising what to do, present a few options neutrally** and let the
  person choose; say which fits best only if asked. This limits advice, not the
  judgment of a motive.
- **Do not push someone to drop a task others depend on,** because those people
  still need it done.
- **Safety comes first; check it before the questions above.** If anyone sounds
  in danger, point at once to emergency services or a local crisis line and a
  qualified mental-health professional. If someone sounds hopeless about life,
  depressed, or low for a long time, suggest a qualified professional. If there
  is no sign of danger and it is unclear whether they mean the task or their
  life, ask once, gently.
