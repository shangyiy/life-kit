# /motivation

Find whether the activity itself moves the person or an outside push does;
outside motives can become reasons the person genuinely holds.

Core check: Would you still do it if the outside push went away?

A yes points to the activity itself or a reason genuinely held. A no means the
push is still outside. A yes driven by guilt or fear does not pass. For someone
else, ask what has happened without the push; do not assume.

## Principles

1. **Name the kind of motive.** Intrinsic motivation comes from the activity's
   interest or satisfaction. Extrinsic motivation comes from reward, pressure,
   comparison, or others' expectations.
2. **Check whether an extrinsic reason is internalized.** Acting only for a
   reward or a demand is external. Avoiding guilt or trying to feel worthy is
   introjected. A reason can be personally important (identified) or fit the
   person's values (integrated). All four remain extrinsic in this framework;
   the last two can still feel like the person's own.
3. **Help the reason become the person's own.** Autonomy gives real choice,
   competence brings growing ability, and relatedness brings connection. These
   needs can help an outside reason become one the person endorses and may
   help it last.
4. **Build self-efficacy.** Self-efficacy is the belief that you can do the
   task. It grows through past success, relevant models, credible encouragement,
   and how physical or emotional signals are read.
5. **Rewards and pressure can crowd out interest.** Expected material rewards
   tied to doing the task, pressure, and controlling evaluation can reduce
   existing interest. Unexpected rewards or positive feedback that supports
   competence are safer.
6. **Use curiosity to begin.** A visible gap between what someone knows and
   wants to know can start action, but it does not create a lasting purpose.
7. **Use structure as support.** Routines, self-set deadlines, clear goals, and
   quick feedback can help action continue. They amplify motivation; they do
   not replace it.
8. **Support choice without engineering feelings.** When asked for help, offer
   reasons the person can examine and structure they choose. Guilt, pressure,
   or deception is manipulation, not support.

## Workflow

Ask the core check instead of inferring its answer. Mixed motives are allowed.
For intrinsic motives, inspect crowding out. For extrinsic motives, name how
far the reason has become the person's own, then inspect needs, self-efficacy,
supports, and avoidance. Aim for an endorsed choice, including dropping the
task. Use only the checks needed for the question.

## Checklist

Did the person answer in their own words? Is any inference marked as
uncertain? Does the support leave a real choice and avoid manipulation?

## Anti-patterns

Treating guilt as endorsement; forcing one label onto mixed motives; giving a
full motive analysis for a planning request; treating avoidance as lack of
care; steering the person toward the helper's choice.

## Output

State the likely motive, whether the core check was answered, and the one
support that matters most. Offer a few short next steps and let the person
choose one.

## Boundaries

Skip this analysis when the person only wants a plan and does not question the
motive. Signs of depression or hopelessness, including losing interest in
everything for weeks, call for care and a suggestion to talk with a qualified
mental-health professional, not a motive analysis. Address immediate safety
first when it is in doubt.
