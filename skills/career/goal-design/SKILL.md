# /goal-design

The use case for designing a career goal through an adaptive interview. Every
principle and definition of [`motivation`](../../core/motivation/SKILL.md),
[`outcome-orientation`](../../core/outcome-orientation/SKILL.md),
[`optionality`](../../core/optionality/SKILL.md), and
[`experimentation`](../../core/experimentation/SKILL.md) applies unchanged.
Those cores explain what moves action, why the result matters, what
alternatives and downside the choice carries, and how to search when no goal is
available. This skill decides how to uncover career motivation, turn it into
candidates, choose and shape one goal, and establish review and stop points.

## Principles

1. **Interview before designing.** Ask one question at a time and build each
   next question from the answer. Find the person's own language, tensions, and
   evidence before proposing a goal. Do not use a fixed questionnaire or ask a
   batch of questions that prevents follow-up.
2. **Uncover the motivation first.** Find what repeatedly draws or repels the
   person, what experiences supply or drain energy, what need the change could
   satisfy, and what makes action feel possible. Resolve vague, inherited, or
   conflicting motives enough to explain why effort would continue. If the
   evidence is thin, use `experimentation` to design bounded discovery rather
   than manufacturing certainty.
3. **Keep source and reason separate.** Establish where each possible goal came
   from and why the person would adopt it. Comparison and relevant, attainable
   models can reveal “why not me?” possibilities. As a heuristic, use near
   peers for concrete paths and distant exemplars for direction. Then use
   `outcome-orientation` to make the why the person's own or discard the
   candidate. A borrowed goal can feel empty when reached or keep moving to the
   next comparison.
4. **Generate alternatives before choosing.** Turn the motivation into several
   contrasting candidate goals, including a bounded exploratory goal when the
   desired direction remains uncertain. Make the candidates concrete enough to
   compare and probe. Do not refine the first plausible answer by default.
5. **Shape candidates for engagement and stretch.** Give each serious candidate
   clear near targets, prompt feedback, and challenge near the edge of present
   skill. Prefer an ambitious, specific direction over “do your best,” but do
   not turn aspiration into a promise. Apply `optionality`: a stretch goal is
   eligible only when its downside is survivable.
6. **Choose explicitly.** Compare the candidates by the wanted outcome, the
   motivation present in their day-to-day pursuit, their opportunity cost, and
   the credible alternatives they preserve or close. State the chosen goal, why
   it won, what it displaces, and the first bounded action.
7. **Set review, stop, and renewal points.** Decide in advance what evidence
   warrants continuing, reshaping, or stopping. Prefer a pursuit that can
   generate a next challenge, and begin the next exploration before a finite
   goal ends. Completion can remove routine, feedback, identity, and direction;
   do not treat a post-goal dip alone as proof that the goal was worthless.
