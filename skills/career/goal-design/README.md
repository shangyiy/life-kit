# goal-design

An adaptive interview for uncovering career motivation, generating candidate
goals, choosing and shaping one, and setting review and stop points
(`/goal-design`).

## Credit

- Principle 2 draws on Edward L. Deci and Richard M. Ryan, “The ‘What’ and
  ‘Why’ of Goal Pursuits: Human Needs and the Self-Determination of Behavior,”
  *Psychological Inquiry* 11(4), 2000, and Kennon M. Sheldon and Andrew J.
  Elliot, “Goal Striving, Need Satisfaction, and Longitudinal Well-Being: The
  Self-Concordance Model,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*
  76(3), 1999, for autonomy, competence, relatedness, and self-concordant
  goals.
- Principle 2 also draws on Bill Burnett and Dave Evans, *Designing Your Life:
  How to Build a Well-Lived, Joyful Life*, Alfred A. Knopf, 2016, for treating
  observed engagement and energy as clues about what to explore.
- Principle 3 draws on Albert Bandura, “Self-Efficacy: Toward a Unifying Theory
  of Behavioral Change,” *Psychological Review* 84(2), 1977, for vicarious
  experience as a source of efficacy expectations.
- Principle 3 also draws on Penelope Lockwood and Ziva Kunda, “Superstars and
  Me: Predicting the Impact of Role Models on the Self,” *Journal of
  Personality and Social Psychology* 73(1), 1997, for the effects of relevant,
  attainable and unattainable role models.
- The near-source and distant-source distinction in Principle 3 draws on David
  A. Kalkstein, Tali Kleiman, Cheryl J. Wakslak, Nira Liberman, and Yaacov
  Trope, “Social Learning Across Psychological Distance,” *Journal of
  Personality and Social Psychology* 110(1), 2016. Applying that distinction to
  career goals is the author's synthesis.
- Principle 4 draws on Herminia Ibarra, *Working Identity: Unconventional
  Strategies for Reinventing Your Career*, Harvard Business School Press, 2003,
  for learning possible directions through action and testing multiple possible
  selves.
- Principle 4 also draws on Burnett and Evans for generating contrasting future
  directions rather than refining only one.
- Principle 5 draws on Edwin A. Locke and Gary P. Latham, “Building a
  Practically Useful Theory of Goal Setting and Task Motivation: A 35-Year
  Odyssey,” *American Psychologist* 57(9), 2002, for specific, difficult goals
  compared with “do your best” goals.
- Principle 5 also draws on Sim B. Sitkin, Kelly E. See, C. Chet Miller,
  Michael W. Lawless, and Andrew M. Carton, “The Paradox of Stretch Goals:
  Organizations in Pursuit of the Seemingly Impossible,” *Academy of Management
  Review* 36(3), 2011, for its theoretical contingency framework: recent
  success and slack resources affect whether stretch goals are likely to help
  or disrupt.
- Principle 5 draws on Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, *Flow: The Psychology of
  Optimal Experience*, Harper & Row, 1990, and Jeanne Nakamura and Mihaly
  Csikszentmihalyi, “The Concept of Flow,” in C. R. Snyder and Shane J. Lopez,
  eds., *Handbook of Positive Psychology*, Oxford University Press, 2002, for
  clear proximal goals, prompt feedback, and challenge matched to skill.
- The renewable-structure distinction in Principle 7 draws on Jesper Juul, “The
  Open and the Closed: Games of Emergence and Games of Progression,” *Computer
  Games and Digital Cultures Conference Proceedings*, Tampere University Press,
  2002. Its application to career goals is the author's synthesis.
- The warning in Principle 7 that a low period can follow completion draws
  cautiously on Karen Howells and Mathijs Lucassen, “‘Post-Olympic Blues’—The
  Diminution of Celebrity in Olympic Athletes,” *Psychology of Sport and
  Exercise* 37, 2018. That qualitative study concerned four Olympians, so it
  supports the possibility of a dip, not a universal claim.
- The one-question-at-a-time interview, its adaptive flow, the
  source-versus-reason framing, the career application of the cited findings,
  and the remaining prescriptions are the author's own.
