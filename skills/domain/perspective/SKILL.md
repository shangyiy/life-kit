# /perspective

The domain of influence: winning someone's support for an idea, a request or a
case. People are moved by their own view of things, so each rule starts from
the other person's side, whoever they are and whatever the medium. Use these to
do the work or to review it; in a review, name each breach once, with its fix.

## Principles

1. **See the situation, and your own words, from the other person's side.**
   People act on how they see things: their goals, their worries, how they
   are measured and rewarded, and what is expected of them. They hear your
   words through that view, not as you meant them. To change a mind, first
   find where it is now. Do not stereotype their world or assume all on
   their side want the same.
2. **Look for the need behind what they ask for.** What they ask for is a
   position; why they want it is the interest. Most interests can be met by
   several positions, so interests leave more ways to say yes. Ask why. Look
   for the basic needs underneath: security, economic well-being, belonging,
   recognition, control over one's life, feeling important.
3. **Be sincere: promise only what you can deliver; praise only what you
   mean.** Flattery and empty promises are counterfeit. People who see you as
   self-serving grow wary and resist; influence over time goes to those
   genuinely interested in others. None of these principles works as a trick.
4. **Offer what they value, not what you value.** Being right is not enough:
   the truth does not win by itself, and competence is only the price of
   admission. An idea moves people only through something they already care
   about, so put it in terms of their interests, not its merit by your measure.
5. **Know what you most need, and stay open on how you get it.** Know what you
   want, what matters most, and what you would settle for. Commit to that need,
   not to one position: firm on the need, flexible on the means, so an answer
   that works for both sides can appear. Under stress people forget their
   primary goal and just want to beat the other side.
6. **Listen first, show you understood, and let them take part in reaching
   the conclusion.** People listen better once they feel understood, so show
   you grasp their interests before asking them to grasp yours. Taking part
   is perhaps the strongest factor in whether people accept an outcome: they
   back what they helped shape, so ask rather than tell where you can.
7. **Be hard on the problem and soft on the person.** A person who feels
   attacked grows defensive and stops listening, even when you are right, and
   once their ego is tied to a position, they defend it; no one
   wins an argument. Judge the substance plainly without softening the verdict,
   but aim it at the problem, not the person. Admit you may be wrong, own your
   mistakes first, let them save face.
8. **Treat the other person as a potential ally toward a shared end, and
   build trust.** Most disagreements are about means, not ends. Do not write
   off someone who resists or is slow to go along; the cause is more often in
   your approach than in them. People help those they trust.
