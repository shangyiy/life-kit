# /perspective

The domain of influence: winning someone's support for an idea, a request or
a case. People are moved by their own view of things, so each principle starts
from the other person's side, whoever they are and whatever the medium. To do
the work, meet each principle. To review an attempt, check it against each
principle in order and name each breach once, with the principle it breaks and
the fix.

## Principles

1. **See the situation from the other person's side.** People act on how they
   see things: their goals, their worries, how they are measured and rewarded,
   and what is expected of them. To change a mind, first find where it is now.
   Do not stereotype their world or assume all on their side want the same.
2. **Look for the need behind what they ask for.** What they ask for is a
   position; why they want it is the interest. Most interests can be met by
   several positions, so interests leave more ways to say yes. Ask why. Look
   for the basic needs underneath: security, economic well-being, belonging,
   recognition, control over one's life, feeling important.
3. **Be sincere: praise only what you mean.** Flattery is counterfeit, and
   people who see you as interested only in your own advancement grow wary and
   resist. Influence over time goes to those genuinely interested in others and
   in the shared goal; none of these principles works as a trick.
4. **Offer what they value, not what you value.** Being right is not enough:
   the truth does not win by itself, and competence is only the price of
   admission. An idea moves people only through something they already care
   about, so put it in terms of their interests, not its merit by your measure.
5. **Know what you most need, and stay open on how you get it.** Know what you
   want, what matters most, and what you would settle for. Commit to that need,
   not to one position: firm on the need, flexible on the means, so an answer
   that works for both sides can appear. Under stress people forget their
   primary goal and just want to beat the other side.
6. **Be hard on the problem and soft on the person.** A person who feels
   attacked grows defensive and stops listening, even when you are right, and
   once their ego is tied to a position, they defend it; no one
   wins an argument. Judge the substance plainly without softening the verdict,
   but aim it at the problem, not the person. Admit you may be wrong, own your
   mistakes first, let them save face.
7. **Listen first, show you understood, and let them reach the conclusion.**
   People listen better once they feel understood, so show you grasp their
   interests before asking them to grasp yours. People trust ideas they work
   out for themselves more than ideas handed to them, so ask rather than tell
   where you can.
8. **Treat the other person as a potential ally, and build trust.** Almost
   everyone cares about something you could offer in return. Do not write off
   someone who resists or is slow to go along: the cause is more often in your
   approach than in them, so look first at what you do or fail to do. People
   help those they trust.
