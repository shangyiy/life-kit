# /perspective

The domain of influence: winning someone's support for an idea, a request or a
case. Each principle starts from the other person's side, whoever they are and
whatever the medium. Use these to do the work or to review it; in a review,
name each breach once, with its fix.

## Principles

1. **See the situation, and your own words, from the other person's side.**
   People act on how they see things: what they care about, how they are
   measured and rewarded, and what is expected of them. They hear your words
   through that view, not as you meant them. To change a mind, first find
   where it is now. Understanding their view is not agreeing with it. Do not
   stereotype their world.
2. **Look for the need behind what they ask for.** What they ask for is a
   position; why they want it is the interest. Most interests can be met by
   several positions, so interests leave more ways to say yes. Ask why. The
   strongest interests are basic human needs, such as feeling important.
3. **Claim only what is true, promise only what you can deliver, and praise
   only what you mean.** People sense flattery and empty promises, and once
   they find one, they start to doubt the rest. Influence over time goes to
   the sincere, those genuinely interested in others. None of these
   principles works as a trick.
4. **Offer what they value, not what you value.** Being right is not enough:
   the truth does not win by itself. An idea moves people only through
   something they already care about, so put it in terms of their interests,
   not its merit by your measure.
5. **Know what you most need, and stay open on how you get it.** Know what you
   want, what matters most, and what you would settle for. Commit to that
   need, not to one position, so an answer that works for both sides can
   appear. Under stress people forget their primary goal and just want to beat
   the other side.
6. **Listen first, show you understood, and let them take part in reaching
   the conclusion.** People listen better once they feel understood, so show
   you grasp their interests before asking them to grasp yours. Taking part
   is perhaps the strongest factor in whether people accept an outcome: they
   back what they helped shape, so ask rather than tell where you can.
7. **Be hard on the problem and soft on the person.** A person who feels
   attacked grows defensive and stops listening, even when you are right, and
   once their ego is tied to a position, they defend it; no one wins an
   argument. Judge the substance plainly without softening the verdict. Admit
   you may be wrong, own your mistakes first, and let them save face.
8. **Treat the other person as an ally toward a shared end, and build trust
   before you need it.** Where you truly share the end, say so: show that
   you differ on method, not purpose. Do not write off someone who resists or
   is slow to go along; the cause is more often in your approach than in them.
   People help those they trust.
